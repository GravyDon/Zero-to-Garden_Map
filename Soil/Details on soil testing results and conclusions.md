## Soil composition for a gardener
 All three basic phases of matter are present within soil (solid, liquid, gas)+ micro-organisms which move and interact chemically and physically with these components (not including plants). The ideal soil will be able to exhibit the following properties:
 1. High amount of surface area for nutrients, but sufficient inter-particle spacing for air and water travel (texture, hygroscopity). 
 2. Ph in acceptable range for plants of interest; some like acidic soils some like slightly alkaline, while most are fine with neutral soils (Ph)
 3. Drainage that is desirable for plants of interest. Drainage should correspond with the desired equilibrium state of the soil after watering (dry, moist, wet) (texture, hygroscopity)
 4. High nutrient content, and suitable mineral content. Perfect soils will also have an equilibrium state that does not make the entire nutrient store soluble all at once but will have an equilibrium state where a safe nutrient and mineral concentration is available and replenished when root uptake occurs (mineral content, nutrient capacity).
 5. Absence of heavy metals and other pollutants (Mineral content).

## Texture test and drawable conclusions
##### Water stratification test
1. Fill a clear container about halfway with soil samples. Preferably a gallon jug or more since you will want to collect samples from multiple locations within your area of interest. Furthermore, the more cubic the form of the soil collected in the container, the easier and more accurate the final results.
2. Fill the container with water and mix. 
3. Wait...
After 1-5 minutes, the proportion of sand will settle out of the solution. Measure the height of this layer. -> Sa_h
After 1-2 hours, the proportion of silt will settle out of the solution. Measure the height of this layer or the total height and subtract the sand layer thickness. -> Si_h
After >24 hours, the proportion of clay particles have settled. Measure the height of this layer (-> C_h) and the total height. -> T

Apply the algorithm to solve for the ratio sand:silt:clay

(Sand %) = Sa_h/T : (Silt %) = Si_h/T : (Clay %) = C_h/T

The ratio of these will indicate which soil type you are working with. This initial test will give you a good idea of the problems that might be faced in conditioning your soil for optimal plant growth.

#### Conclusions
IF you have a high clay soil, it is imperative to ensure that the soil has good drainage, has a good microbiome to increase nutrient transport capabilities, and you work in some amendments or pioneer plants to break up and aerate the soil (especially important for rainy months when the clay particles will expand and push out air pockets). High clay soils, while challenging for their chemical properties when experiencing dry spells (cracking) and large amounts of rainfall (mud), their water and nutrient retention is superior when properly managed.

IF you have sandy soils, it is imperative that you choose the plants that can survive in this soil, or else you will be watering very frequently for diminished returns. This is because the drainage rate is very high resulting in poor moisture retention. Furthermore, nutrient capacity will not be as high as well as problems with leeching. Amending the soils with biochar or other materials that can retain and disperse nutrients is essential. High sand soils will rarely experience problems with rot, soil compaction, flooding, cracking, or erosion (except in instances with high wind or flooding), and can support unique species that are drought tolerant.

IF you have silty soils, beware of erosion and compaction. It is imperative to not tread or over water these soils due to soil compaction and erosion respectively. However, out of all the biases, higher silt content will result in better availability of water in the soil layer and good nutrient retention.

Loams are the perfect texture with little issues depending on the two highest numbers in the ratio. You will experience the benefits and detriments of those two soil constituents more than the third component.

## Ph test and conclusions
The Ph test is simple. You can use the results of the water stratification test to measure the Ph. You can use a strip or meter to measure the Ph while the mineral content of the solution is still suspended (within the first few hours of the test). 

There also exist Ph meters that can be stuck directly into the soil. Note that the presence of plant matter (particularly roots) will change the Ph reading so it is important to stick the meter directly into the soil a few inches under the surface and to test multiple locations at different depths where the root layer is not predominant.

#### Conclusions
Nutrient uptake is dependent on the Ph. Certain compounds are more available depending on the Ph. Refer to the following diagram; plant related diseases involving nutrient deficiency can be exacerbated depending on soil Ph at planting or its change dependent upon the introduction of fertilizers, microbiota, or soil amendments (such as wood ash or biochar).

## Nutrient Capacity (CEC) and conclusions

The nutrient capacity of a soil or biochar is an essential property in determining the fertility of the soil. It's quantitative measure is that of cation exchange capacity (CEC for short) and implicates soil as an anion binding site (i.e. having an abundance of negatively charged morphology). As a curious note, it's much more obvious that this is the case since a positive bias would imply ionization of the soil which is a higher energy state than an abundance of electrons in soil lattices. 

Of the three distinct soil particle types, sand, silt, and clay, the differences between them in expected CEC are from lowest to highest respectively. Clay then has more negative binding sites than sand, with silt being an in-between. Organic matter has even higher CEC potential; this is expected considering carbon structures are very good at conducting and holding electrons as well as creating structures with high surface area.

CEC is thus affected by the available surface area, particle size, and chemical composition, which all translate to the real metric of the number of negatively charged binding sites. CEC is such an important metric, there are countless papers evaluating available methodologies, with almost every study of fertility or soil quality featuring CEC measurements correlated with an independent variable.

#### Testing CEC conventionally
Testing CEC can be done through multiple different ways either through chemical analyses or physio-electrical
1. Use a chemical species that can "outbind" the ions in the current binding sites of the soil sample ((NH4)SO4 for example), then measure the molarity of extracted ionic species. Further analysis can be done by extracting using another ionic species then finding the molarity of the first ionic species.
2. Use methylene blue or some other suitable dye and observe the absorptive capabilities. Note saturation tone and tone after period of absorption by soil sample. Spectroscopy can be used to quantify absorption spectra and find the molarity of methylene blue absorbed by negative binding sites. [Article on methodology](https://edu.rsc.org/download?ac=12668)

#### Low cost experiment to observe CEC (perhaps with unintended benefits)
The experiment that I am proposing here to analyze the CEC of biochar is simple, but could provide false results if it doesn't go exactly as planned.
The procedure is facilitated through the process of electrolysis, and the steps are as follows:
1. Measure the Ph of the soil prior to experimentation. This will be a calibration point to determine what ions are already present that would effect the final Ph of the electrolysis solution. The measuring of Ph needs to be calibrated to the initial pH of the sample. The calculations for this involve a dynamic equilibrium. If the pH of the soil is already low, then the experiment will not create a large shift in the pH of the sample, but might do so in the solution, but only because the ionic species of the sample have been replaced with hydrogen ions. 
2. Insert sample into an electrolysis tank. Measure the initial pH of the tank with a pH of 7 being ideal for test conditions.
3. Turn on electrolysis; wait X hours as the sample absorbs aggressively binding hydrogen ions. This will leave the hydroxide ions in solution affecting the Ph. Hydrogen gas that is produced should be cycled back into the tank via a pump and water emitter to capture hydrogen into hydronium (H30+)
4. Extract sample and measure Ph of the solution. The basic properties of the hydroxide ions directly correlate with the acidic properties of the sample now. Ph+POH = 14 -> pH change of solution is directly proportional to the pH change of the sample. Correct for the initial pH to account for the Ph of the solution changing as a result of ion transfer from the soil sample to the solution upon protenation (hydrogen ion binding).
5. A direct measurement of the soil sample Ph can also be done, but it will require decoupling the hydrogen ions from the soil sample which will not be easy and require a binding medium stronger than the existing sample or some other process. In this case, it is better to use other methods of CEC testing.

##### Potential snags
1. If the hydroxide ions recombine with the hydrogen ions on the binding sites of the soil at a fast rate, saturation of the sample with hydrogen ions cannot happen. Hydroxide would have to recombine with another ionic species to allow for the experiment to occur which could change Ph readings, but change them at a predictable rate.
2. If there are significant losses of hydrogen in gaseous form, or there is significant additions to the system from hydrogen external to the tank, then the pH measurements will not reflect 1:1 exchange with the soil and electrolyte.
3. The electrolyte will have to have ionic species in it to create a current, but this ionic species cannot decompose or be reactive with species of ions coming off of the soil sample. Furthermore, the voltage applied needs to create hydronium and hydroxide without enabling other reactions to occur.
4. The electrolysis process is inefficient or cannot run for the required time to reach saturation.
5. Hydroxide ions are absorbed by the soil sample.

An added benefit of this process is that it will open the door to using electrolysis for chemical manufacturing too! This will be highly beneificial if done correctly, but can also be quite useless too.

For biochar, the process is simplified in terms of the Ph calibration since it is intrisically alkaline
