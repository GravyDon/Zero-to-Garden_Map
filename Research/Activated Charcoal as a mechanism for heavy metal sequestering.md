#### Note on heavy metal bio-availability in soil as ion species

So the issue of heavy metals becoming *bioavailable* and being absorbed into plant tissues is an issue of immobilizing these contaminants and eventually processing them via pyrolysis or (plasma arc) gasification into usable metallic substances, energy, and forms that are generally not going to be susceptible to exchange across root-soil boundaries. 

Normally, soils have the innate capability to sequester cationic forms of metals due to its natural charge. the most common forms that metals are found in are amorphous oxides and hydroxides, and to a lesser extent carbonates, phosphates, sulfates, and sulfides, which are relatively insoluble (not bioavailable; immobile). Aluminum, antimony, barium, beryllium, cadmium, nickel, silver, cobalt, ==copper==, ==iron==, lead, ==manganese==, and ==zinc==all can complex with inorganic soil constituents to form cationic species. The energetic favorability of any particular reaction depends on Ph. These metals (usually) precipitate or exist as cations which can be sequestered by the negatively charged soil strata, but **does this lead to long term stability of these elements? Some of these on the list are essential micro-nutrients for the soil meaning that there is a more complex process happening here that allows for uptake of metallic ionic compounds.** ==(highlighted metals are essential for plant life cycle)== Cobalt is involved in legume function in nitrogenizing the soil. Silver nanoparticles or colloidal silver can be used to enhance plant function but is not necessary for plant life cycles[^1]

On the other hand, arsenic, chromium, selenium, and vanadium typically exist as anionic species -> This is applicable to uptake in biochar due to activated characoal's porosity allowing for the trapping of anionic species by complexing with oxygen.

#### Role of activated charcoal in the sequestering of anionic species of cadmium and chromium 
From the study provided in the source, there are two clear effects on heavy metal bioavailability due to the presence of activated charcoal:
- Direct sequestering of elements due to the binding of anionic species (**The exact mechanism of sequestering was not illustrated just statistical analyses of control and experimental groups in terms of cadmium and chromium content**)
- Increasing of Ph which can affect the bioavailability of some nutrients (**can be a very bad thing for acid-loving plants**) -> Does *biochar* have a lower Ph shift per gram due to the presence of microbes and other nutrients?

Additionally, the substance is beneficial for sequestering more significant nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorous), water, and carbon dioxide for the sake of overall plant health.

Study conclusion:
```quote

In conclusion, using 2% biochar as an organic amendment for contaminated soils can be an effective method for improving soil quality and promoting plant growth. Biochar amendments can reduce the concentrations of toxic heavy metals in soil, increase soil nutrient levels and chlorophyll contents, improve gas exchange attributes, and improve soil water holding capacity and aeration. However, the appropriate application rate and timing of biochar amendments should be carefully considered to avoid unintended consequences. Further research is needed to determine the optimal application rate and long-term effects of biochar amendments on soil quality and plant growth in contaminated soils.
```
