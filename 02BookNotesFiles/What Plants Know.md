This book is focusing on the sense perceptions of plants, and how they respond to those sense perceptions. The operative word "know", instead of the human equivalent of being able to communicate concepts into action or educative value, is rather a demonstration of the plant's ability to adapt to its environment. 

Whether these plant adaptations are what is necessarily best for the plant is another question; such a question depends on the ecological viability of the plant which is outside of the scope of the book and more in the scope of an actual garden. Instead, the book aims to relate human equivalent senses, and their associated sensory inputs, with plant senses both for deeper appreciation and kinship with plants as well as a practical understanding of how plants develop when exposed to certain sensory loads.

### Sensory input of Light
##### List of terms: 
- #Phototropism: the proclivity of plants to bend towards the light, no matter how dim, This is for the sake of getting more light clearly. 
- #Photoperiodism : the tendency for certain plants to change their behavior depending on the **length of a continuous period of darkness** OR a measure of how much light is being taken in over a daylight cycle. The distinction made here is in line with experimentation showing a change in plant behavior dependent upon artificial light affecting plant behavior despite the amount of light being significantly shorter (thus showing that the mechanism associated with this behavior is an ON/OFF switch with lag time)

Phototropism was discovered in an experiment in 1864 Darwin and his son demonstrated the localization of function for phototropism is in the tip of canary grass. Whether this is always the case is circumspect, but it can be reasonably believed that such localization of light sensitivity is common in the early stages of plant growth considering that every plant grows from a shoot where only the tip is liable to find available sunlight. **Julius von Sachs discovered that plants phototropic response is stimulated primarily by blue light.**

Photoperiodism was first experimented with because of a commercial tabacco variety called Maryland Mammoth not going to flower. This variety was planted in a pot and periodically placed in the shade for part of the day. This caused it to flower, thus indicating a response to darkness in the middle of prime light hours. This response is present in irises, soybeans, and likely many other plants (perhaps all of them to some extent). **unlike phototropism, red light is used to indicate the length of the day (which makes sense since the sunset is red-shifted).** 

	Thus, blue light indicates the direction of the sun for a plant and red light indicates the length of incident light on the plant. 
	Even more interestingly, far-red light, which is commonly seen at dusk, cancels out the effect of red light on flowering response. The amount does not have to be significant, but this means that there is an off switch to photoperiodism in the form of far-red light, and it is truly like a switch. The plant will not distinguish a sequence of red and far-red pulses, measuring out the total to discern the rate of higher exposure. Instead, it will "remember" the last red wavelength (either nominal or far) and use that to judge its "light situation" and subsequent flowering or leaf production response. This can be used to counteract premature flowering in the event of an unfortunate stimulus coming at an untimely moment in the season. 

**Additionally, the leaves of a plant contain the protein responsible, phytochrome, that morphologically shifts with the perception of red light or far-red light, with red light required to shift its sensitivity to the far-red light and vice versa.** This essentially acts like a switch for when the sun rises (red light; up and at em!) and when the sun falls (far-red light; time to chill). However the flowering or leave proliferation response depends on the plant. 

#technique/Photoperiodic_forcing : it is possible to delay or force the life cycle of plants by simply flicking incident lights at night. This can destabilize hormonal signals in the plant allowing them to either stay stuck in a specific stage (such as chrysanthemums which flower in the fall but are made to flower in the Spring) or to force flowering (such as with Irises). This could also be a sign of light pollution or lack of light wavelengths causing maladaptive behaviors.

Finally, both humans and plants share an evolutionary commonality in perceiving blue light: **cryptochromes**. These proteins indicate to us signals that can affect circadian rhythms in animals as well as day-and-night cycle behaviors in plants. 
This is from photoperiodism primarily due to the fact that it is in response to blue light ONLY, so the timing of plant functions seems to be tied to both red light and blue light, but the functions that each have depend on the plant. However, it is almost always true that plant metabolism shifting to the flowers is from exposure to sufficient levels of red light.
