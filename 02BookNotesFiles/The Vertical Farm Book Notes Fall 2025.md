So far (at page 153) I'm convinced that this is truly the solution for agriculture on a scale that is national or even global that is needed. While I don't think this book detracts from the ecologically sound solutions that are offered via sibling::[[Integrated Foresty Book Notes Fall 2025]], I'm still skeptical of the scalability of such an time and knowledge intensive solution that requires so much planning and knowledge of plant and soil dynamics in so much vertical space, ecosystem management, and flexible responses towards different challenges in the landscape, climate, and natural shifting of the biomass due to natural processes. Of course, there is a place for both books in a new world past the current agricultural decline (more on that below). Certainly, this book presents the most competitive, scalable, economic, and uniquely poised solution to face the current challenges of the climate crisis, fouling of our soils through heavy salts (much worse than the situation of the dust bowl which ravaged American agriculture and also a much different situation in terms of globalized systems of trade and the overall food needs of America and beyond), poisons in the form of herbicides and pesticides destroying local ecologies, and the lack of studious and fastidious ownership of land by families and small companies by conglomerates that are more interested in not growing anything or only the most high yield crops on the market via high-capital, expensive methods. 

Urban farming practices which rightly use the vertical space provided, which is an ingenious premise squished between "the" and "farm", solves multiple issues present because of the current farming industry:
- Accessibility of viable growing space for local farmers, hobbyists, restaurants, and other urban and suburban parties in need (even though suburban shouldn't exist) **(Food deserts in cities gone and education)**
- Current usage of horizontal space for agricultural needs **(Ecological restoration efforts revitalized)**
- Transportation costs and usage of gas-guzzling farm equipment **(fossil fuel usage goes down along with food prices)**
- Ecological solutions as a result of inclusion of plants in the cycle of waste management:
	- Commodification of food waste (completing the cycle; usage of waste as fuel for more food) **(Less financial stress on households, restaurants, grocery stores, and perishable food storage solutions)**
	- Processing of gray water via uptake in plants in vertical farms, transpiration, and dehumidification. Potential for plant based solutions in processing of black water with infrastructure to break down solid waste. **(Sustainable solution to processing of waste water)**
- Less strain on extraction from LDC's/peripheral countries for agricultural goods **(preservation of global south resources)**
- Zero need for pesticides or herbicides **(Less need for environmental pollution)**
- Substitute for the impending agricultural crisis due to groundwater pollution and penetration of soil with salts, pesticides, and Ph disruptive molecules **(Preparation for the impending crisis of feeding people)**
- Less water waste **(70-95 percent less water usage depending on the method of hydroponics or aeroponics used)**
- Greater food safety and overall security of our nations food sources

The author argues that the lessons to be learned from history are found within the vertical farm. Unlike in the dust bowl, there will be no ecological destruction without a knowledge of the workings of the climate in relation to preserving natural resources. Unlike in Bangladesh and India, freak accidents in nature (flooding and hail) will not dictate who is feed and who is not. Currently, a tragedy is unfolding in the San Joaquin Valley or central valley of California where the groundwater is being irreparably damaged via ecologically unsound irrigation practices. This building catastrophe is going to cause a mass starvation event and the loss of 60 billion dollars in revenue for California. 

The history of farming and its forces up to today is also interesting with the presentation of the four main factors that led to the modern system of farming (outside of the first and second agricultural revolution) intrigue me. First is the civil war (and the system of chattel slavery associated with its contention even though the real problem was the production of cotton and textiles by the south and north, respectively, and the competition between getting the better end of the deal), next is the discovery oil and the invention of the internal combustion engine, and finally dynamite for clearing out agricultural space with native practices damned straight to hell. 

Consequences of a successful implementation: 
- Co-opting by capital (ofc)
- Population cap for a space increases (is this necessarily a good thing?) -> If integrated into global trade, then the global population cap increases
- Redundancy of traditional farming methods (extraction of people and capital from rural areas without and effective subsidiary solution or co-extensive solution to enriching rural folks)

Some of these problems will go away when there is no need for capital to dominate the extent of human action, but that day remains to be found.

There are four main principles to the design of a vertical farm that are key to success:
1. Capture sunlight and disperse it evenly among the crops
2. Capture passive energy for supplying a reliable source of electricity
3. Employ good barrier design for plant protection
4. Maximize the amount of space devoted to growing crops
