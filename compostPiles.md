### Construction considerations
The first step is to plan out the space that you want the composting pile. Depending on the size of the pile, you can get a higher compression on more material. The more compressed the compost is in the pile, the better that micro-life can thrive in the environment since their next meal is so close, but you also will have to aerate the pile past a certain point of compression. if you use lime and inoculants, you can make the compost pile burn faster and thus have a higher wattage, but again you will have to aerate the pile. 

Ratio of "browns" to "greens" in a compost pile is another factor to consider. This is referring to the carbon to nitrogen ratio which, in general, does relate the color of the inputs into a compost pile. The ideal ratio is 60:30 as the carbon will make up the majority of the microbes biological structures, but nitrogen will be necessary for protein creation. Additionally, adding more nitrogenous material tends to accelerate decomposition while more carbon contributes to greater microbial mass. 

Breakdown of the carboniferous material is dictated entirely by bacterial metabolism, both aerobic and anaerobic within different layers of the compost pile. Inoculation upon initialization of the compost can not only speed up the decomposition rate, but act as a great soil inoculant without much fuss since the bacteria/fungi will have a transitory nutrient source while migrating into the soil for long-term shelter.

Summary: 
- Bacteria and fungi drive the decomposition process 
- Maintaining a 60:30 ratio of carboniferous material to nitrogenous material in a compost will allow for a fast decomposition process since both materials are involved in proliferating microbial populations and activity respectively.
- Compression of compost creates a higher metabolic activity of bacteria
- Compost can be used as a soil inoculant, nutrient source, 

### Compost pile 1: chicken wire cage
One of the most simple and quickly assembled compost piles, that is also resistant to uneven surfaces, is achieved through the usage of chicken wire and some stakes. The stakes height will determine the potential height of the compost pile. Make sure that the chicken wire is at a height suitable to support the compost pile AND that the pore/hole sizes in the wire are both small enough to prevent compost falling out and large enough to allow for ample air penetration into the sides of the compost. 

##### Construction
```tabs
--- Materials
1. Chicken wire 
   choosing the required area (m^2 or ft^2) is dependent on the perimeter of the compost pile and the height (outer surface area). There is no need to be exact just ballpark it or purchase an ample amount.
2. Wooden stakes 
   Height of the stakes should be greater than 4ft to get a compost pile with ample compression. 
3. Fasteners (screws and wire)
   

**Tools: hammer, impact drill, wire manipulators (needle nose pliers and slip-joint pliers), and a pitchfork**

--- Methods
1. Pound in the stakes to a suitable depth. The height of the stakes does not need to be equal, but the stake at the minimum height should be suitable for the maximum height of the compost pile.
2. Fix the chicken wire to the stakes using screws and wire. The process might be tedious, but it will be well worth the effort. Using both pliers, manipulate the wire to wrap around screws drilled into the wooden stakes. TO create tension. Grab a portion of the wire and twist. Secure the twist using more wire or through rethreading the wire loop through a screw.
3. Layer the wire to a suitable degree. If the height of the roll is not to the height of the pile, layer to reach the height requirement.
4. After the wire, providing a cardboard or cellulose bottom will be beneficial to prevention of nutrients seeping into the soil and keeping a tilth to keep moisture levels high. If this step is seen as not preferred, then simply add compost.
   
--- Initializing. 
The compost pile should be layered with carboniferous (brown) and nitrogenous (green) material. Below is a table providing examples of carboniferous and nitrogenous material. The pile should not be compressed too much as this will create anaerobic conditions which will create a poor smell and make the breakdown of the pile slower overall when exposed to air. If aeration is a concern, turning over the pile with a pitchfork or sturdy stick is possible. If the pile is too large and wide, consider installing an aeration pipe.

An aeration pipe will allow for air to enter the compost pile; it will provide enough structure and negative space for air to permeate into the center of the pile. 

--- Extra: creating aeration pipe
Materials: 
- 1 inch PVC (max height of pile + width of pile + enough pipe to go from center of pile to 1 foot out of it)
- 1 inch PVC tee/cross/5 or 6-port connector (the more ports, the more offshoots for the pipe)
- Leafblower, bellows, or dakota fire hole
- 1 inch elbow connector
- Fasteners (stakes, flange that will fit 1-inch pipe, zipties)
- (optional) one or two 1 inch PVC end caps for both ends of the main aeration pipe
  
Create a PVC structure that will span the height of the pile, branch out towards the edges of the pile (depends on the PVC tee, cross, or other connector gotten). Drill small aeration holes on the bottom and sides of the branching pipes (if using active aeration) and on the top side relative to where the compost will sit on the branch pipe. On the main pipe, drill holes all along the perimeter. 

Add a cap if using bellows or a manually operated air pump for aeration, cap the main pipe to build pressure and add penetration into the more compressed parts of the pile. If using a dakota fire hole, provide an offshoot for the elbow at the bottom of the aeration pipe to pull air into the fire (coming from the top of the aeration pipe and through the array into the fire). The same elbow chute coming from the top of bottom of the aeration pipe is also required for the other aeration methods.

Fasten the pipe into the ground using the flange as a connection between the pipe and the fasteners; isolate the flange using zipties through holes in the pvc. or some other method.
```

# Fact: you can heat a bathtub full of water using the power of bacterial metabolism

A compost pile, through the activity of the molds, bacterias, and small detrivores in the leaf pile, the metabolism of these lifeforms produces heat as lost energy. Smaller forms of life lose heat like crazy; their smaller size and lack of space to metabolize means that they have to be simpler and thus have more simplistic stores of energy. Larger mammals have great stores of energy and can preserve them well. 

Because of this, massive stores of heat are produced by smaller life due to their inefficiencies of scale allow for the water within a #tool/heatingCoil to come in to extract said heat. To produce the ideal amount of heat, the flow rate or the decomposition speed can be adjusted; the former is more easily modified while the latter requires the addition of more nitrogenous material and can only be increased (decreasing would require culling of the pile or the microbial populations to a needless degree).
