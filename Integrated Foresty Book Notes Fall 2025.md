Initial impressions:
	This book provides a wealth of information on plant cultivation from a scale that approaches the complexity of stewardship within a "natural" (but of course anthropocentrically derived) garden with various interconnections between plants, fungi, animals, insects, bacteria, and the weather. It's focus is mainly on the considerations that are required, from a high level, in the creation of an "integrated forest": a space that is inhabited by the seven layers of plant strata which harvest energy and recirculate vertically and horizontally across the cultivated space and along the margins. The end goal of the book is to maintain the knowledge to be able to design your own plant guild, follow a procedure to implement said plant guild in a way that makes sense for the rate of succession (how fast do other plants need to be present to support each other in their growth process?), and then properly maintain and implement them for personal, communal, and ecological needs.
	The amount of knowledge in this book is vast; i'll have to review it, but for now I can provide a list of considerations that are needed when designing a plant guild

A plant guild needs the following functions to be fulfilled:
1. Dynamic accumulators: ==Aggregating minerals, such as potassium, calcium, phosphorous, boron, zinc, molybdenum, and more for the sake of being used as a mulch or green manure for other plants to uptake in their growing process. Provides minerals in bio-available forms==
	- Potassium will assist with yields during blooms/fruiting periods in the growing season
	- Calcium helps with the cell walls and ion exchange in the plant. Supports leaf and stem health
	- Boron...
	- Potassium...
	- Zinc...
	- Molybdenum...
2. Nitrogen Fixers: ==Aggregating nitrogen either in the root structure (legumes) or in the leaves, these plants provide bio-available forms. This essential molecule is in **all proteins**, so it's importance in development cannot be understated. 
3. Nursery plants: ==During the early development of plants, there are various forms of predation and environmental damage that can jeopardize the longevity of the plant. This is where the role of nursery plants comes in to either ward off pests, shelter the plant from wind and heavy rains, or provide key nutrients/root interactions for growth. One could consider the willow tree a nursery plant for its root growth hormone that can be used on all plants!
4. Insectary: ==Insect habitat==
5. Food/medicine/dye/fiber/etc source (animals and plants) ==Self-explanatory==
6. Mulch Accumulator: ==Example would be a tree or other shrub that drops a lot of plant material for decomposition==
7. Water preserving: ==Dependent on root structure AND the switching between photosynthesis (which usually happens during the night actually) and transpiration==
8. Drought resistant: ==Good to include considering climate change; preserves water resources during drought conditions==
9. Soil mender: ==There are cases where a site is unable to support most plant species due to soil conditions such as the presence of toxic salts, poor soil texture, toxins, or over-fertilization. In such cases, there exist plants that can extract these harmful chemicals and either convert them into more bio-available forms for nutrient conversion or to completely get rid of them through dislocation to another site. For example sunflowers can extract many different heavy metals from the soil which can mess with soil texture and root function (plus lead!!)==
10. Pioneer plant: ==Generally plants which spread quickly and in almost any condition; good property for ground cover which also supports another ecological function==
11. Any more?

Types of plants (in ascending order of height):
1. Ground cover
2. Herbaceous plants
3. Shrubs & bushes
4. Hedges
5. Vines
6. Understory Trees
7. Overstory trees

Balancing these layers for the intake of sunlight, while also realizing that the inclusion of one layer will reduce the sunlight reaching a layer below is an essential consideration. You do not have unlimited space AND layering so as to allow for all plants to attain their necessary amount of sunlight and water is essential to survival. 

Most importantly, plans will change. One plant that was included in the design might be too aggressive and end up shading out another plant.  Perhaps disease spreads from a species that is not planted in a soil that is well aerated enough.
